# Continual Scientific Learning

## Goal

Cohervia Scientist should improve by accumulating scientific memory and revising theory, not by silently rewriting its objective or governance controls.

## Durable memory

The minimum persistent scientific memory is:

- **Theory graph** — current constructs, relationships, evidence states, and version.
- **Prediction ledger** — predictions made before outcomes are known.
- **Failure ledger** — mistakes, falsifications, null results, and postmortems.
- **Anomaly ledger** — observations the current theory cannot adequately explain.
- **Research-history ledger** — later addition for hypothesis strategies and experiment outcomes.

## Learning from mistakes

A failure is not complete until the Scientist records why it believes the result occurred and what assumption it changes.

The postmortem should distinguish:

1. the observed outcome;
2. the interpretation;
3. the confidence in that interpretation;
4. the affected theory elements;
5. unresolved alternatives;
6. follow-up experiments capable of discriminating between those alternatives.

## Prediction calibration

Predictions are recorded before experimental outcomes are available.

Over time the Scientist should evaluate:

- calibration;
- directional accuracy;
- boundary-condition errors;
- repeated failure modes;
- overconfidence;
- underconfidence.

The objective is not to maximize prediction accuracy by avoiding difficult predictions. Prediction records exist to expose where the theory is weak.

## Anomaly pressure

Unresolved anomalies should not be prematurely normalized into the dominant theory.

An anomaly may be:

- simulator artifact;
- measurement failure;
- unmodeled state;
- theory failure;
- distribution shift;
- unknown.

Repeated anomalies should increase research priority.

## Theory revision

Theory updates are proposals with provenance.

A revision must identify:

- previous theory version;
- evidence motivating the change;
- contradicted evidence;
- changed nodes/edges;
- expected consequences;
- regression risks;
- experiments that could falsify the new revision.

## Regression discipline

A new mechanism is not scientific progress merely because it improves a new benchmark.

Candidate revisions should later be evaluated against:

- prior supported effects;
- known adversarial regimes;
- prior falsifications;
- boundary measurements;
- untouched confirmatory holdouts where eligible.

## Research-process learning

The Scientist may analyze its own research history to identify patterns such as:

- repeated hypothesis classes that fail;
- experiments with poor discriminative power;
- recurring leakage risks;
- unnecessary model complexity;
- high-value anomaly classes;
- hypothesis strategies that produce useful information.

Any change to research strategy remains subordinate to the Scientific Constitution.
